Safety Recalls — Verify First

Rear axle bolt fracture — 22V-486 (CRITICAL)

Critical
Rear axle pinion bolt can fracture under load, allowing the rear axle to separate from the vehicle. This is a catastrophic failure mode that caused accidents. NHTSA investigation resulted in Recall 22V-486.
Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls BEFORE any test drive. If 22V-486 shows as open/incomplete, do not drive the vehicle. Walk away or require dealer remedy before proceeding.
Est. cost: Free dealer repair under recall — or walk away Source: NHTSA recall 22V-486

Transmission

10R60 transmission shudder / software

High Attention
The 10-speed auto on the Explorer has TSBs for shudder, harsh downshift, and hunting in stop-go traffic.
Test in stop-and-go at 15–30 mph for 5+ minutes. Shudder during light acceleration is torque converter shudder or software issue.
Est. cost: $350–$550 fluid flush + calibration / $4,500–$7,000 torque converter Source: Ford TSB 20-2157

Powertrain

2.3L EcoBoost coolant consumption

Monitor
Some 2020–2021 2.3L EcoBoost engines have reported gradual coolant loss without external leaks — suspect head gasket or degas bottle.
Check coolant level cold before starting. Any loss between services = suspect head gasket. Check for white exhaust on warm engine.
Est. cost: $1,800–$3,500 head gasket Source: NHTSA complaints

3.0L EcoBoost water pump (internal)

High Attention
The 3.0L Platinum/ST uses an internal water pump driven by the timing chain. Failure allows coolant to contaminate oil — catastrophic engine damage.
Verify coolant color and oil appearance on 3.0L engines. Any mayonnaise on oil cap = coolant intrusion. Check service history for water pump replacement.
Est. cost: $2,500–$4,500 internal water pump Source: Ford 3.0 Cyclone known failure pattern

Electrical

SYNC 4 / infotainment issues

Low Risk
SYNC 4 is generally better than prior generations but has known freeze and connectivity issues on early 2020 production.
Test all infotainment functions including wireless CarPlay/Android Auto.
Est. cost: $0 software update Source: Ford software campaigns

What the VIN tells you

8th VIN digit engine code on the 6th-gen U625 Explorer: ‘H’ = 2.3L EcoBoost turbo four, ‘C’ = 3.0L EcoBoost turbo V6, ‘B’ = 3.3L naturally aspirated V6. All three were confirmed by decoding a constructed 2020 Explorer VIN through NHTSA vPIC with position 8 as the single variable and everything else held constant, and vPIC returned 2.3L/4-cylinder, 3.0L/6-cylinder and 3.3L/6-cylinder in turn; characters outside that set return error 14. Two controls are worth stating, because they are what keeps the claim narrow and checkable: changing position 9 left the engine at 2.3L, confirming that digit is the check character and not powertrain data, and changing position 7 moved the reported trim from XLT to Limited while the engine held, so trim and engine are encoded separately. This digit matters more than the model year on a 2020 Explorer, because the three engines fail differently. ‘H’ is the volume engine: listen for cam-phaser rattle on a cold start and put the coolant-intrusion question to its service history. ‘C’ is the ST and Platinum engine, so it is also the car most likely to have been driven hard — look for heat-stressed turbo plumbing and intercooler pipework, and treat a seller with no records as a reason to walk. ‘B’ is the simplest of the three mechanically, but it is not the one to relax on, because the 10-speed automatic behind it and the rear axle are where this generation’s real risk sits. Whichever character you read, this generation carries a critical rear-axle bolt-fracture campaign, 22V-486, so run the full VIN at nhtsa.gov before any test drive and do not drive the vehicle if it shows as open or incomplete. One limit of this digit: a 3.3L hybrid was also sold, and its displacement reads the same as ‘B’, so displacement alone will not separate a hybrid from the gas V6 — confirm electrification from the full-VIN decode rather than from position 8.
