2015 Honda CR-V
LX / EX / EX-L / Touring (4th gen, 2012–2016 — 2015–16 are the post-refresh cars)
Low Risk

The 4th-gen CR-V (2012–2016) is a dependable compact SUV, but 2015 is a hard split inside that generation: the mid-cycle refresh swapped the port-injected K24Z7 and 5-speed automatic for the direct-injected K24W and a CVT. A 2015 or 2016 car is a CVT car — if a listing or a shop quotes you 5-speed automatic service on one, they are working from the wrong half of the generation. The standout issues on the refreshed car are CVT judder (Honda Service Bulletin 15-046) and windshield stress fractures. A significant share of 4th-gen CR-Vs develop a crack pattern along the base or sides of the windshield without impact. Honda issued TSB 14-005 but declined to extend a recall. Buy one without a crack, inspect thoroughly, and verify the A/C evaporator for mold. Note: the 5th gen (2017+) switched to a 1.5T turbo with an oil dilution problem far worse than anything on the 4th gen — stick with 2012–2016 if you want the simpler, proven drivetrain.

Engine & Transmission

K24W Earth Dreams engine — overall health check

Low Risk
The K24 is one of Honda's most durable four-cylinders with a timing chain (no belt to replace). Documented weakness is oil consumption on very high-mileage units or cars with stretched oil change intervals.
Pull the dipstick and check oil level — should be at or near full. Check oil condition on a white paper towel: very black = old oil; metallic sheen = bearing wear. Cold-start and listen for chain rattle in the first 5 seconds — should be quiet.
Est. cost: $0 if maintained / chain service rare on K24 Source: Honda K24 engine documentation

CVT judder and low-speed vibration (2015–16) — Honda SB 15-046

Monitor
The 2015 refresh replaced the 5-speed automatic with a CVT, and a meaningful share of 2015–16 cars developed a vibration or judder — Honda Service Bulletin 15-046 sorts it into three modes: stopped in gear, light acceleration around 1,000–2,000 rpm, and a 40–50 mph cruise at roughly 1,800–2,200 rpm. The fix is a PCM/software update plus a fluid change, and Honda extended coverage on the emissions-related portion. Honda HCF-2 fluid only — generic CVT fluid will cause shudder on its own. (A 2012–2014 car is a different transmission entirely: the port-injected K24Z7 with a 5-speed automatic on ATF-DW1.)
Drive it from cold: sit in gear at a stop and feel for a pulse through the floor, then accelerate gently from 15 mph and hold a steady 45 mph. Any shudder that comes and goes with light throttle is the SB 15-046 pattern. Ask whether the software update and fluid service were done, and get the RO number — this is verifiable at a Honda dealer against the VIN.
Est. cost: $150–$250 HCF-2 fluid service and software update / $3,000–$5,000 CVT replacement if neglected Source: Honda Service Bulletin 15-046 (2015–16 CR-V vibration while driving and/or stopped in gear)

Windshield & Visibility

Windshield stress fractures (TSB 14-005)

High Attention
This is the defining defect of the 4th-gen CR-V. Stress cracks initiate at the base of the windshield (near the cowl/wiper area) or along the lower side edges — often without any impact point. Honda issued TSB 14-005 but declined to issue a formal recall. Replacement windshields can crack again in the same location if the root cause isn't addressed.
Inspect the windshield from inside in bright light — look specifically at the bottom 6 inches and both lower corners. A crack pattern without a clear impact point (no bull's-eye, no star) is a stress fracture. Do not accept a seller's claim that "it just happened" — check if any replacement glass is OEM Honda.
Est. cost: $300–$700 windshield replacement (may recur) / no Honda recall reimbursement Source: Honda TSB 14-005 + NHTSA complaint data

Backup camera fogging (EX / EX-L)

Monitor
Backup cameras on 4th-gen CR-Vs develop fogging or degraded image quality from water intrusion into the camera housing over time.
Reverse with the camera active and inspect image clarity. Fogging inside the lens, persistent condensation, or a washed-out image = camera housing seal failed.
Est. cost: $150–$350 camera replacement Source: Honda CR-V 4th gen camera complaint reports

HVAC & Electrical

A/C evaporator mold smell (Honda-wide pattern)

Monitor
Honda's HVAC evaporator design allows the evaporator to stay wet during short trips, creating mold — usually strongest when A/C first turns on after sitting overnight.
Start cold and run A/C on max fan — smell the vents immediately. A musty, locker-room smell = evaporator mold. Common but not a mechanical failure — more of a comfort issue.
Est. cost: $100–$300 evaporator treatment / $800–$1,500 replacement if severe Source: Honda A/C evaporator mold pattern (widely documented)

Rear wiper motor failure

Monitor
Rear wiper motors on 4th-gen CR-Vs develop internal gear wear and fail to sweep a full arc, stopping midway or parking off-center.
Activate the rear wiper and watch: full sweep stop-to-stop, clean pause, and return. Hesitation, partial sweep, or grinding = motor failing.
Est. cost: $150–$350 rear wiper motor (DIY-accessible) Source: Honda CR-V 4th gen owner reports

Keyless entry range issues

Low Risk
The smart-entry module can fail, causing intermittent key-fob range issues or door-unlock delays. Most commonly a dead fob battery — rule that out first.
Test the key fob at 30+ feet from the car. Doors should lock/unlock reliably. Walk all the way around. Weak range = try a new CR2032 battery first, then investigate the module.
Est. cost: $10 fob battery / $200–$400 module if needed Source: Honda keyless entry module reports

Power liftgate struts (EX-L)

Low Risk
Power liftgate struts on EX-L trim lose tension with age and fail to hold the hatch fully open, especially in cold weather.
Open the liftgate fully — hands off — and let it rest for 60 seconds. If it slowly drifts down, struts are tired. Cold weather test is most revealing.
Est. cost: $80–$180 strut pair (DIY) Source: Honda CR-V EX-L liftgate strut reports

Suspension & Brakes

Front strut mount bearing wear

Monitor
Strut mount bearings wear by 80,000–100,000 miles, causing a popping or groaning sound during low-speed turns — especially in cold weather.
In a parking lot, turn the steering wheel lock-to-lock slowly at idle. Any grinding or popping from the front strut towers = strut mount bearings worn.
Est. cost: $300–$600 strut mount pair Source: Honda CR-V 4th gen strut mount wear pattern

Rear brake caliper sticking

Monitor
Rear disc brakes develop sticking calipers, usually from infrequent parking brake use. A stuck caliper causes premature rear pad wear and a pull under light braking.
After a highway drive, carefully feel the radiated heat from each rear rotor (don't touch — just hover your hand near the wheel opening). One rear dramatically hotter than the other = stuck caliper on the hot side.
Est. cost: $200–$400 caliper rebuild or replacement per side Source: Honda CR-V rear caliper sticking — common pattern

Brake pedal feel and rotor condition

Low Risk
CR-V brake pads wear at a normal rate. Factory rear pads are smaller and wear faster than fronts.
Brake firmly from 40 mph. Pedal should be firm and consistent. No pulsation = rotors flat. Ask for brake service history.
Est. cost: $200–$400 brake service (pads + rotors front or rear) Source: Standard CR-V maintenance

NHTSA Recalls (verify completion via VIN)

Multiple
Open recalls — VIN check (not a Takata car)
Honda's CR-V Takata scope is 2002-2011 — the second and third generation cars. The RM 4th-gen CR-V is not in any Takata inflator campaign, so ignore a listing that advertises the repair. Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for the campaigns that do apply and require every open one closed before purchase.

What the VIN tells you

Honda puts the engine character in the 5th VIN digit, and on this car it is a trap: a 2015 CR-V reads ‘M’ — but so does a 2013 and a 2014, because the same 5th-digit code carries across the whole 4th generation even though the engine underneath changed mid-cycle. The 5th digit cannot tell you which 2.4 you are looking at. The 10th digit — the model-year character — is what settles it: D = 2013, E = 2014, F = 2015, G = 2016. F or G is the refreshed car: direct-injected K24W (185 hp / 181 lb-ft) with a CVT. D or E is the port-injected K24Z7 with a 5-speed automatic. Two halves of one generation, two different inspections, two different service quotes. On an F or G car, ask about Honda Service Bulletin 15-046 (CVT judder) and demand a CVT fluid record — Honda HCF-2 fluid is not interchangeable with the 5-speed’s ATF-DW1, and a shop that put the wrong fluid in is a walk-away, because a replacement CVT is typically quoted in the $3,500–$5,000 range installed. On a D or E car every CVT check on this list is meaningless. Positions 4–6 carry the chassis code and give you the drivetrain: RM3 is the front-drive 2.4, RM4 is all-wheel drive — the AWD car adds a propeller shaft and rear differential the front-drive car does not have, and rear-diff fluid is a real service item on it. Never accept “it’s a 2015-ish CR-V” from a listing; decode the full VIN at autovetting.com/decode before you apply this checklist.
