2014 Toyota Highlander

Low Risk

The 3rd-gen Highlander (XU50) is one of the most refined and reliable three-row crossovers on the used market, but buy the right half of it: the 2014–2016 car uses the port-injected 2GR-FE (270 hp) with the U660E six-speed, and the direct-injected 2GR-FKS and eight-speed only arrive with the 2017 facelift. That matters because most of the carbon-buildup advice written about this Highlander describes the later engine and does not apply here. On a 2014 the inspection is genuinely low-drama — confirm the 14V-576 fuel delivery pipe recall has been remedied, check the timing cover and water pump for seepage, test the panoramic roof drains, and you are largely done.

Power Train

Powertrain

2GR-FE V6 — port injected, so no GDI carbon problem

Low Risk
The 2014 Highlander V6 is the 2GR-FE: 270 hp, port fuel injection, one of the most durable engines Toyota has built. Fuel sprays across the back of the intake valves on every cycle and keeps them clean, so the walnut-blast decarbon service quoted for this model online is not a 2014 expense — that advice belongs to the 2017-and-later 2GR-FKS. What this engine does do at high mileage is seep oil, usually from the timing cover or the water pump.
With the engine warm, look down the front of the block at the timing cover seam and at the water pump (passenger side, low). Dried oil crust or wet coolant residue means a seep already in progress. Also pull the dipstick: clean amber or light brown, at the full mark.
Est. cost: $400–$700 water pump / $1,200–$2,000 timing cover reseal Source: Toyota 2GR-FE service pattern; MY2014 powertrain verified against Toyota specifications

2GR-FE oil consumption — minor concern

Low Risk
The 2GR-FE is not an oil burner by reputation, but at high mileage some examples use a quart per 3,000–5,000 miles, usually through valve stem seals. Not a class-action-level issue, but worth checking on a car with 120k+ miles.
Pull the dipstick and check the oil level relative to the last reported change mileage. If oil is near the MIN mark and the car was recently serviced, investigate further.
Est. cost: $0 if minor / $3,000–$5,000 ring or valve work if severe Source: Toyota 2GR-FE owner reports

U660E six-speed automatic — smooth and durable

Low Risk
The 2014 V6 uses the U660E six-speed automatic (the eight-speed arrives with the 2017 facelift). It is a well-proven unit and failures are uncommon, but fluid condition matters and Toyota calls it lifetime fill, which in practice means nobody changes it.
Verify ATF was serviced per Toyota schedule. On a test drive, confirm all shifts are smooth through the full speed range. Any shudder at light throttle (30–45 mph) is a sign of fluid degradation or torque converter wear.
Est. cost: $220 ATF service / $3,500–$5,000 rebuild if neglected Source: Toyota U660E reliability documentation

Brakes & Suspension

Brake pad and rotor wear

Monitor
The Highlander is a 4,300–4,500 lb crossover. At typical used-market ages (6–10 years), original rotors are likely due or past due.
Visually inspect pad thickness through the wheel spokes. Pad should be at least 4mm. On the test drive, confirm no pulsation, shimmy, or grinding. Ask for service records for brake history.
Est. cost: $400–$700 front brakes (pads + rotors) / $700–$1,100 all four corners Source: Standard wear item

Front struts and rear shocks — age check

Monitor
At 7–10 years of age, original struts and shocks on the XU50 Highlander are at or near end of expected life, particularly on city-driven examples with lots of small impacts.
Bounce each corner — should rebound once cleanly. Listen for clunking over slow-speed dips. On a highway test drive, check for float or excessive body roll.
Est. cost: $700–$1,200 front strut pair / $400–$700 rear shocks Source: Standard wear item

Sway bar end link rattle

Low Risk
Front sway bar end links rattle over bumps at low speeds — a known minor wear item on this generation Highlander.
Listen for a metallic click or knock when rolling over parking lot speed bumps slowly.
Est. cost: $80–$160 end links (both sides) Source: Toyota Highlander XU50 owner pattern

Body, Interior & Electrical

Panoramic moonroof drain tubes

Monitor
The available panoramic roof on XLE and Limited trims has four drain tubes that exit at the rocker panels. Debris can clog them, causing water to pool and leak into the headliner or footwells.
Pour a cup of water into each front drain channel and watch for it to exit at the corresponding rocker panel. Also press on the headliner near the roof opening for dampness.
Est. cost: $80–$150 drain cleaning / $1,000+ if headliner or carpet is water-damaged Source: Toyota XU50 moonroof drain pattern

Power liftgate struts and motor

Low Risk
The optional power liftgate can slow or fail to open fully when strut assist gas has depleted. Motor and latch issues are uncommon but do appear on high-mileage examples.
Cycle the liftgate open and closed three times from both the button and the key fob. It should travel smoothly and latch firmly.
Est. cost: $150–$350 struts / $300–$600 liftgate motor Source: Standard wear item

Third-row seat fold and seatbelt condition

Low Risk
On family SUVs the third row is often stored folded and rarely tested. Fold/unfold mechanisms can stiffen or bind.
Deploy and stow the third row completely. Test all seatbelts — click and release should be clean.
Est. cost: $50–$200 latch repair Source: Standard wear item

Entune infotainment — responsiveness

Low Risk
The Entune infotainment is dated by modern standards but reliable. 2017+ units received Entune 3.0 with improved performance.
Tap through all menus and test Bluetooth pairing. Response should be under 1 second per tap.
Est. cost: $200–$600 head unit replacement if unresponsive Source: Toyota Entune documentation

Dashboard trim rattle (minor)

Low Risk
Some XU50 Highlanders developed minor rattles from dashboard panels and trim clips, particularly around the center console area.
On a test drive, listen for rattles on rough pavement. Press on dashboard trim to locate any panels that respond with a click.
Est. cost: $0 (clip re-seat) to $50 (replacement clips) Source: Toyota Highlander XU50 owner reports

Recalls & Safety

Fuel delivery pipe leak (14V-576) — VERIFY COMPLETE

Critical
The 2014 Highlander shares a fuel-delivery-pipe recall with the 2014 Avalon, Camry and Sienna: fuel can leak from a pipe in the engine compartment, next to hot exhaust. This is the one campaign on this truck that can burn it down. Note that the 2014 Highlander is NOT part of the Takata inflator recall, whatever a listing may claim.
Enter the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before seeing the vehicle. Any open recall = do not purchase until the dealer remediates it for free. Do not accept a verbal assurance. With the engine warm, also smell the engine bay for raw fuel and look for staining around the fuel rail and delivery pipes.
Est. cost: Free dealer repair — demand completion before purchase Source: NHTSA campaign 14V-576 (Part 573 report)

NHTSA Recalls (verify completion via VIN)

14V-576
Fuel Delivery Pipe Leak (fire risk)
2014 Highlander, Avalon, Camry and Sienna plus the 2015 Lexus RX 350: fuel can leak from one of the fuel delivery pipes in the engine bay. A fuel leak next to hot exhaust is a fire risk. Dealers replace the suspect pipe free — verify by VIN.
14V-272
Passenger Seat Belt Force Limiter — Airbag ECU Software
2014 Highlander and Highlander Hybrid: the airbag ECU shipped with software that applies the restraint setting intended for a roughly 110-pound occupant to every front passenger regardless of size (FMVSS 208). Free software update.
14V-274
Second-Row Right Seat Not Locking
Narrow campaign — only 2014 Highlanders built April 23 through April 29, 2014. The second-row right-hand seat may not fully lock into the inboard seat track in its forward adjusting positions (FMVSS 207).
14V-051
Third-Row Middle Seat Belt Anchorage
2014 Highlanders built November 20, 2013 through January 18, 2014: the third-row middle seat belt may not have been properly secured to the floor anchorage during assembly (FMVSS 210). Inspect and secure, free.
18E-107
Aftermarket Replacement Windshield — ECM Water Damage
An equipment recall, not a Toyota campaign: Fujian Wanda replacement windshields sold for 2014-2018 Highlanders carry a wire harness that water can leak into, damaging the engine control module and stalling the engine. Only relevant if the glass has been replaced — ask, and check whether the windshield carries Toyota etching.

Open recalls — VIN check
The 2014 Highlander is NOT part of the Takata inflator recall. Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls anyway — the campaigns that do apply here are build-date scoped — and require every open recall closed before purchase.

What the VIN tells you

The 5th VIN digit carries the engine on a 2014 Highlander and it resolves a three-way split that changes what you are buying: ‘A’ is the 2.7L 1AR-FE four (185 hp, front-drive base LE only, dropped after 2016), ‘K’ is the 3.5L 2GR-FE V6 (270 hp) behind the U660E 6-speed and the volume engine, and ‘C’ is the 2GR-FXE in the Highlander Hybrid. The hybrid is the one that matters most to identify, because on a 2014 it was sold only in Limited and Limited Platinum trim — the same badges as a gas V6 Limited — so once the window sticker is gone the trim tells you nothing. A ‘C’ means an eCVT and a nickel-metal-hydride traction pack, and a pack replacement typically quotes $2,500–$4,000 depending on reman versus dealer-new. Price that in before you fall for a hybrid’s fuel numbers, and have the battery’s state of health read rather than inferred from how the truck feels on a 10-minute test loop. An ‘A’ four-cylinder is the other one to catch deliberately: front-drive only, the slowest 3rd-gen Highlander by a wide margin, and the wrong truck if you tow — buyers who wanted a V6 and never checked the 5th digit are the most common regret on this generation.
