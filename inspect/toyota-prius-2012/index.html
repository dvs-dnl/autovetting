Two / Three / Four / Five (ZVW30, 3rd gen 2010–2015)
Low Risk

The 3rd-gen Prius (ZVW30) is one of the most reliable used cars in any segment — 200,000+ mile examples are common with minimal powertrain drama. The hybrid system itself is robust. Three things require non-negotiable attention at this age: (1) catalytic converter theft is epidemic on Priuses — confirm the car still has one before you do anything else; (2) the HV battery pack is 9–13 years old and deserves a capacity check; and (3) the inverter water pump uses a plastic impeller that fails silently and can destroy the inverter if neglected. The rest is straightforward used-car inspection.

Hybrid System

HV battery pack — state of health

High Attention
The ZVW30 uses a 201.6V NiMH HV battery. At 9–13 years old, capacity degradation is measurable. A weak pack causes the ICE to cycle more frequently, reducing fuel economy and adding engine wear. Severe cases trigger a red triangle warning and limp-home mode. The factory warranty covered HV battery to 10yr/150k in CARB states, 8yr/100k federally.
On a warm drive, watch the energy flow display — if the HV battery consistently shows only 4–5 bars of charge range (not 8), it is degraded. A Toyota-compatible scan tool (Techstream or BlueDriver with hybrid data) can pull State of Health data. Replacement packs are $1,500–$3,500 installed new, or $600–$1,200 refurbished.
Est. cost: $600–$3,500 (refurb vs. new) Source: Toyota HV battery warranty documentation; Prius Chat forums; owner complaint patterns

Inverter cooling water pump

High Attention
The hybrid inverter is liquid-cooled. The pump uses a plastic impeller on early ZVW30 builds that corrodes and fails — often silently. Without coolant flow, the inverter overheats. Inverter replacement is $3,000–$5,000+. This is the most expensive surprise repair on a 3rd-gen Prius.
Park on level ground and listen: with the car in Ready mode (but not moving), the electric pump should hum faintly near the front of the engine bay. A rattling, grinding, or absent sound is a red flag. Check the inverter coolant reservoir (separate from the main coolant) — should be clean and at the correct level.
Est. cost: Pump: $200–$400 installed / Inverter if damaged: $3,000–$5,000+ Source: NHTSA complaint database; Prius community (priuschat.com)

HV battery cooling fan — cabin air filter path

Monitor
The HV battery is cooled by a fan that draws cabin air through an inlet behind the rear passenger seat. This fan collects lint, pet hair, and debris over time. A clogged or seized fan causes the battery to run hot, shortening its life. Many owners never clean it.
Ask if the HV battery cooling fan has ever been cleaned or replaced. With the car in Ready mode, lean your head near the rear seat — you should hear a faint fan hum when the battery is warm. Check the inlet grille for debris.
Est. cost: $0 (DIY clean) to $150–$300 (fan replacement) Source: Toyota maintenance documentation; Prius Chat community

Catalytic Converter

Catalytic converter — theft verification

Critical
Prius catalytic converters contain unusually high concentrations of platinum, palladium, and rhodium — at peak precious-metal prices, a Prius cat was worth $700–$1,500 on the scrap market. Theft became epidemic 2019–2023 and remains common. A stolen cat causes an immediate loud exhaust roar and multiple CELs. Replacement is $2,500–$4,000+ at dealer prices. Confirm the cat is physically present before anything else.
Walk around to the passenger-side front of the car and look under the engine bay with a flashlight — the catalytic converter should be visible as a metallic canister on the exhaust pipe upstream of the main pipe. Start the car: if it sounds like a motorcycle or hot rod, the cat is gone. A VIN-etched anti-theft shield on the underside is a good sign the car is protected.
Est. cost: $2,500–$4,500 dealer replacement; $800–$1,500 aftermarket Source: NHTSA complaint database; NICB theft data; CarFax theft reports

Engine & Drivetrain

ICE oil consumption — 2ZR-FXE

Monitor
The 1.8L 2ZR-FXE runs in Atkinson cycle, which keeps intake valves open longer — effective for economy, but can allow some oil to be drawn into the combustion chamber at high mileage. Consumption is generally mild (less severe than Subaru or Honda 1.5T) but worth checking on high-mileage examples.
Check the oil dipstick for level and condition. Oil should be golden-amber, not black. Ask when the last oil change was and compare oil level to dip. If oil is dark and below MIN, walk away.
Est. cost: $0 (maintenance) to $1,000+ (piston ring job on severe cases) Source: Toyota owner reports; Consumer Reports reliability data

Brake actuator — spongy pedal under hard stops

Monitor
Early 2010 ZVW30 Priuses were subject to a brake actuator software recall that affected brake feel under certain low-speed ABS conditions. 2012–2015 models are not affected by that recall but the electro-hydraulic brake system can develop a spongy feel as the actuator ages.
At low speed (10 mph), apply firm brake pressure — the pedal should feel firm with no sponginess or unexpected ABS activation. One firm stop from 60 mph should also feel normal.
Est. cost: Software update: free / Actuator: $1,200–$2,500 Source: NHTSA recall database; Toyota TSB documentation

Transaxle fluid — lifetime claim not realistic

Monitor
Toyota marketed the Prius transaxle fluid as lifetime — but at 12+ years and 100k+ miles, the fluid degrades and has been linked to transaxle noise and shudder on high-mileage examples. A fluid change is inexpensive insurance.
Ask about transaxle fluid history. A responsible previous owner will have changed it at 100k. If fluid has never been changed on a 100k+ car, factor $150–$300 into your offer.
Est. cost: $150–$300 at an independent shop Source: Toyota dealer service bulletins; Prius Chat long-term ownership threads

Interior & Technology

Touch-screen / MFD display — delamination

Monitor
The center multi-function display on 2010–2015 Priuses can delaminate — a haze or "bubbling" appears between the display layers, most visibly in sunbelt states. It is cosmetic but distracting and costly to replace.
View the center display from multiple angles in bright light — look for clouding, hazing, or a "oil slick" appearance within the screen layers. Minor edge delamination is common and negotiating leverage.
Est. cost: $300–$700 for refurbished display Source: NHTSA complaint database; Prius owner community

Auxiliary 12V battery

Monitor
The Prius has a small 12V auxiliary battery (in the trunk, under the cargo floor) that powers accessories and the HV system startup. This battery typically lasts 4–5 years. Many used Priuses are on their second or third 12V — an aged or weak 12V causes random error messages and can prevent the car from entering Ready mode.
Ask when the 12V auxiliary battery was last replaced. Check manufacture date on the battery label (under cargo floor). A battery over 4 years old is a near-term replacement.
Est. cost: $200–$350 for OEM-spec AGM 12V Source: Toyota documentation; Prius Chat community

Exterior & Body

Headlamp lenses — UV hazing

Low Risk
The Prius headlamp lenses are prone to UV oxidation and hazing, especially in sunbelt states. This is cosmetic but reduces light output noticeably.
Inspect headlamp lenses in daylight for yellowing or clouding. Polishing kits ($20–$40) resolve mild hazing; severe cases need lens replacement ($150–$400/pair aftermarket).
Est. cost: $20–$40 (polish kit) to $300–$600 (replacement lenses) Source: Common cosmetic issue; owner forums

Rear hatch struts

Low Risk
The large rear hatch struts weaken over time. At 10+ years, the hatch may drift down when open.
Open the hatch fully and release — it should hold position without support. Struts are $40–$80/pair DIY.
Est. cost: $40–$80 (DIY) Source: Common wear item; owner community

Recalls & Safety

Open recall campaigns — verify via VIN

Monitor
The 2012–2015 Prius has been subject to multiple recall campaigns over its life. The most significant for the ZVW30 include the inverter Intelligent Power Module (14V-053, 2010–2014 Prius — can cause loss of hybrid power and stall), seat belt pretensioner (select builds), and various software campaigns. All should be complete before purchase.
Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before purchase. Any open campaigns should be completed at a Toyota dealer at no cost before you sign.
Est. cost: Free dealer repair Source: NHTSA recall database
