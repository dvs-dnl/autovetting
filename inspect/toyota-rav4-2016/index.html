Low Risk

The 4th-gen RAV4 (2013–2018) is one of the safest used-SUV picks available — the 2AR-FE engine runs to 200,000+ miles with basic maintenance and Toyota reliability holds firm. The Takata airbag recall does not touch this car — Toyota’s RAV4 inflator campaign covers the 2004–2005 model — so the real checks are elsewhere. Backup cameras fail or fog on high-mileage examples, and liftgate hinges rattle. Otherwise you're buying a well-documented, easy-to-maintain commuter with an excellent parts supply.

Engine & Powertrain

Open recalls — VIN check (the RAV4 Takata recall is not this car)

Monitor
Toyota ran exactly one Takata driver-inflator campaign on the RAV4 and it covers the 2004–2005 model — the second-generation car. The XA40 is not in it, and Toyota does not list any RAV4 under its passenger-inflator campaigns either. Treat any listing that advertises a completed Takata repair on a 2016 RAV4 as a sign the seller is reading someone else's checklist.
Look up the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before purchase. Any open campaign = do not buy until the dealer has closed it at no charge. Completion is logged against the VIN, so verify rather than take the seller's word.
Est. cost: Free under recall Source: Toyota Takata affected-model list (toyota.com/recall/takata), verified 2026-08-09

Oil level and consumption (2.5L 2AR-FE)

Monitor
The 2AR-FE is generally oil-tight, but some owners report gradual consumption on high-mileage units or vehicles used primarily on short trips. Not as severe as Subaru's EJ-series, but worth verifying.
Pull the dipstick with the engine cold. Oil should be at or near "full." If it's 1+ quart low between changes and the engine is over 100,000 miles, track oil level between fill-ups before committing.
Est. cost: $0 if normal / engine wear concern if chronic Source: Toyota 2AR-FE owner reports

Timing chain (2AR-FE — no belt required)

Low Risk
The 2AR-FE uses a timing chain, not a belt — no scheduled replacement. The chain is extremely durable. Occasional startup rattle reported at very high mileage (150,000+) when oil changes have been neglected.
Cold-start and listen for any chain rattle in the first 5 seconds. Should be silent within 2 seconds of startup. Sustained rattle = chain wear from oil neglect.
Est. cost: $800–$1,500 chain service if worn (rare under normal maintenance) Source: Toyota 2AR-FE reliability documentation

AWD rear coupling (AWD models)

Low Risk
Toyota's RAV4 AWD uses an electronically-controlled rear coupling — generally reliable, but mismatched tire sizes (different diameters front vs. rear) can bind and wear the coupling prematurely.
Verify all four tires are the same size and brand. In an empty lot, drive a slow tight circle — no binding or clicking. Engage AWD Lock if equipped and confirm it releases cleanly.
Est. cost: $800–$2,000 rear coupling if worn Source: Toyota RAV4 AWD system documentation

Electrical & Technology

Backup camera failure and fogging

High Attention
Backup camera failure is the #1 NHTSA complaint for 4th-gen RAV4. Water intrusion causes fogging inside the lens, progressive image degradation, or complete failure. Most common on 2013–2015, less frequent on 2016+, but worth checking on any example.
Shift to Reverse and evaluate the backup camera image. Look for fogging, condensation inside the lens, washed-out color, flickering, or black screen. Test in changing temperature if possible — fogging often appears after cool overnight sits.
Est. cost: $200–$450 camera replacement Source: NHTSA complaint data — top complaint category for 4th gen

Power door lock actuator failure (rear doors)

Monitor
Rear door lock actuators — particularly driver-rear — are a common failure point. The actuator motor strip-gears or fails to engage. The door still opens manually, but power locks become unreliable.
Lock and unlock all four doors from every control point: door buttons, key fob, and driver's master switch. Listen for the click of all four locks moving. A click with no movement = failing actuator.
Est. cost: $150–$300 actuator replacement (DIY-friendly) Source: RAV4 4th gen owner forums

Pre-Collision System calibration (PCS-equipped 2016+)

Low Risk
Toyota Safety Sense P (TSS-P) was optional/standard on 2016 RAV4. The radar and camera require recalibration after windshield replacement or front-end collision repairs.
Verify any PCS or collision-warning lights on the dash are not illuminated. Ask about windshield replacement history — if replaced, confirm recalibration was done at a Toyota dealer.
Est. cost: $200–$400 dealer recalibration after windshield work Source: Toyota TSS-P system requirements

Body & Interior

Liftgate hinge rattle

Monitor
Rear liftgate rattle is endemic to the 4th-gen RAV4 — the hinge pivot points develop slop and the weatherstripping around the liftgate aperture compresses with age. Results in a rattle or thump over rough pavement.
Drive over a rough surface with rear windows closed and listen. A rattle from the rear = liftgate hinge. Push on the liftgate from inside the cargo area — any give or movement confirms looseness.
Est. cost: $50–$200 weatherstripping / $300–$600 if hinge replacement needed Source: RAV4 4th gen owner reports (widespread pattern)

Windshield stress cracks

Monitor
Less common than on the Honda CR-V, but 4th-gen RAV4 windshields are reported to develop stress fractures at the base of the glass or near the wiper cowl area, especially in cold climates.
Inspect the full windshield in direct sunlight from inside looking out. Check the lower edge and both lower corners for any crack pattern without an obvious impact point.
Est. cost: $300–$700 windshield replacement / $50–$150 chip repair if minor Source: RAV4 4th gen windshield complaint reports

HVAC blower and cabin air filter

Low Risk
Cabin air filters are often skipped and can become so clogged they restrict airflow and create mold odor. The blower motor itself is durable.
Set the fan to max and feel for strong, even airflow across all vents. Musty smell on max fan = clogged or moldy cabin filter. Ask for cabin filter service history.
Est. cost: $20–$40 cabin filter (DIY) / $100–$200 if evaporator treatment needed Source: Common HVAC maintenance item

Suspension & Brakes

Front strut wear at high mileage

Monitor
OEM RAV4 struts are tuned for comfort and wear relatively quickly — many examples over 80,000 miles show front-end float or nose-dive under braking.
Press firmly on each front corner and release — one controlled return (no bounce). More than one bounce = strut worn. Highway drive: steering should feel planted, not vague or wandery.
Est. cost: $600–$1,000 front strut pair (OEM) / $400–$700 aftermarket Source: RAV4 4th gen suspension wear pattern

Brake rotor warping

Low Risk
RAV4 rotors are adequate for normal use but warp from repeated aggressive braking (mountain driving, towing). A pulse in the pedal during highway deceleration is the tell.
Brake firmly from 60 mph on the highway. Any pulsation or shimmy through the steering wheel = warped rotors.
Est. cost: $250–$500 rotors + pads (front or rear) Source: Common brake maintenance

What the VIN tells you

Toyota encodes the engine in the 5th VIN digit, not the 8th. ‘F’ = 2.5L 2AR-FE gasoline four (176 hp, 6-speed automatic, front-wheel drive or mechanical AWD with a driveshaft and rear differential). ‘J’ = 2.5L 2AR-FXE hybrid — 2016 is the RAV4 Hybrid’s first U.S. model year, so a 2016 is the earliest one that exists: 194 hp combined, eCVT, and AWD-i, which drives the rear wheels with a separate electric motor instead of a driveshaft. This checklist is written for the ‘F’ gas car, and three of its checks simply do not exist on a ‘J’ hybrid: there is no propeller shaft, rear differential, or transfer case back there, and the eCVT has no conventional fluid-and-clutch service history to ask about. Replace them with hybrid-specific checks. Cheapest and most important: find the traction-battery cooling intake vent behind the rear seat and confirm it is not blocked by cargo, a seat cover, or pet hair — restricted cooling airflow is the leading cause of premature pack degradation, and it costs nothing to look. Then scan for stored hybrid-system DTCs before you drive it. The stakes are the reason: the 2016 traction battery carried an 8-year/100,000-mile warranty (the 10-year/150,000-mile term applies only to 2020-and-newer RAV4s), so every 2016 is out of coverage, and a replacement pack runs roughly $3,000–$8,000 new or $1,500–$3,500 refurbished. Never take a seller’s “it’s all-wheel drive” as evidence of which car you are looking at — gas AWD and hybrid AWD-i are different hardware with different inspections.
