The 2nd-gen LEAF is a practical and affordable EV for city and suburban use. The main buyer concern is battery degradation: the LEAF uses passive thermal management (no active liquid cooling) which accelerates capacity loss in hot climates or with frequent DC fast charging. CHAdeMO rapid charging is available but the standard is being phased out. Check battery health carefully — a used LEAF in Phoenix is very different from one in Seattle.

Battery Health — Critical Check

Battery capacity bars — check all 12

High Risk if lost bars
LEAF shows capacity via 12 battery bars in the instrument cluster. Each lost bar = about 6.25% capacity lost. A 2019 LEAF that has lost 2+ bars has significant degradation.
Check instrument cluster — all 12 bars should be present on a well-maintained sub-50k example. Count them explicitly.
Est. cost: $5,000–$8,000 battery pack replacement if severely degraded Source: Nissan LEAF battery bar system

Climate and charging history

High Risk in hot climates
LEAF batteries degrade faster in hot climates (Arizona, Texas, Florida, California) and with frequent DC fast charging.
Request Carfax for registration state history. Southern-state or high-DCFC-use cars may show 3–4 lost bars by 60k miles.
Est. cost: n/a Source: LEAF passive cooling degradation pattern

What the VIN tells you

On the 2019 LEAF the battery is not in the 8th VIN digit — it is in the 4th, and that one character is the whole buying decision on this car. NHTSA's own pattern data separates them: a VIN beginning 1N4AZ decodes with OtherEngineInfo 'Motor (BAT:40kw)' (the 40 kWh car, 150 hp, roughly 150 miles of range), while 1N4BZ decodes 'Motor (BAT:62kw)' — the 62 kWh LEAF Plus (214 hp, roughly 215–226 miles). Confirmed by single-variable decode at vpic.nhtsa.dot.gov and anchored on a real-world Plus VIN, 1N4BZ1CP1KC319895, which decodes error-0 clean to 62kw and is listed by the selling dealer as an SL PLUS. Changing only the 8th digit (P to U) leaves the battery reading unchanged, so do not try to read capacity there. Two cautions: NHTSA writes the capacity as 'kw' where it means kWh, and no vPIC field reports battery health — the 12-bar capacity gauge in this checklist is still the only way to learn what a hot-climate car has left.
