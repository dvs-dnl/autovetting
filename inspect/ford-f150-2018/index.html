Engine / Drivetrain

Engine — Cam Phasers (3.5L EcoBoost + 5.0L Coyote)

Cam phaser rattle on cold start — 3.5L EcoBoost V6 (highest risk)

Critical
The 3.5L EcoBoost’s variable cam phaser system is the single most-reported mechanical issue on the 2018 F-150. Onset as early as 40,000 miles has been documented. Continuous rattle at warm idle — not just the first 2–3 seconds of cold start — means the phasers are already failing. Ford TSB 22-2200 covers phaser replacement on the 3.5L EcoBoost. Cam phaser failure on the 3.5L is a $3,000–$5,000 repair.
Start the engine fully cold (overnight sit ideal) and listen at idle for metallic ticking from the front of the engine for the first 5–10 seconds. Two to three seconds of cold-start tick then silence = normal. Sustained tick beyond 10 seconds, or any rattle at warm idle = phasers failing. Sellers sometimes pre-warm trucks — ask explicitly if the engine is cold.
Est. cost: $3,000–$5,000 cam phaser replacement (3.5L EcoBoost) — Ford TSB 22-2200 Source: Ford TSB 22-2200 + NHTSA complaint aggregation + F150ecoboost.net forum data

Cam phaser rattle on cold start — 5.0L Coyote V8

High Attention
The 5.0L Coyote V8 shares the cam phaser design concern. Oil-change intervals matter most: stretched changes past 7,500 miles are the most consistent predictor of early phaser wear. Pull OBD codes — P0022, P0021, P0012, P0011 are cam timing/phaser fault codes.
Cold-start and listen from the driver’s-side front of the engine. A 2–3 second tick that clears = normal Coyote behavior. Rattle persisting beyond 5 seconds, continuing into warm-up, or audible at highway speed = phaser wear. Verify oil change records.
Est. cost: $2,000–$4,000 cam phaser replacement (5.0L Coyote) Source: Ford 5.0L Coyote cam phaser pattern + NHTSA complaint data

5.0L Coyote plastic oil pan leak

High Attention
The 2018 5.0L Coyote uses a two-piece plastic oil pan with a documented seal-failure pattern. Leaks develop at the gasket seam. OEM patch repairs often re-leak; many shops replace with an aftermarket aluminum pan for a permanent fix.
On a lift, inspect the oil pan gasket seam at the front and rear of the pan. Wet seepage or crusted black residue at the seam = active or prior leak. Fresh RTV at the seam = prior patch attempt (higher recurrence risk).
Est. cost: $400–$1,200 depending on OEM patch vs. aluminum aftermarket pan Source: Ford 5.0L Coyote oil pan seal complaint pattern

5.0L spark plug ejection risk at 100k+ miles

High Attention
The 5.0L Coyote aluminum cylinder heads have relatively few threads for the spark plug seat. A plug never torque-checked with anti-seize can eject when disturbed, destroying head threads and turning a $40 part into a $500–$2,500 repair. Trucks past 100,000 miles with no documented plug service are a material risk.
Ask about plug service history on any 5.0L past 80,000 miles. A careful change with anti-seize and proper torque spec should be documented. No record + 100k+ odometer = budget for a careful plug change before driving hard.
Est. cost: $200–$400 careful plug service / $500–$2,500 thread repair if plug ejects Source: Ford Modular V8 spark plug ejection pattern (non-trivial on aluminum head at high mileage)

2.7L EcoBoost — well-regarded, verify timing belt service

Monitor
Unlike the 2015 first-year model (which had a documented head gasket defect), the 2018 2.7L EcoBoost has a solid track record. Its main maintenance item is the wet timing belt running in the oil bath — Ford specifies replacement at 150,000 miles but many mechanics inspect earlier. Cam phaser complaints are less frequent than on the 3.5L.
Verify oil change history — the timing belt runs in oil, so sustained clean oil matters. Check for cam phaser rattle at cold start (same test as 3.5L). Confirm timing belt service if the truck is past 120,000 miles.
Est. cost: $600–$1,200 timing belt service / $1,500–$3,000 if phasers also needed Source: Ford 2.7L EcoBoost production documentation + 2015 vs. 2018 model year comparison

10R80 Ten-Speed Automatic Transmission

10R80 shudder and harsh shifts (30–45 mph)

High Attention
The 10R80 ten-speed automatic (introduced on the 2018 F-150 across all but the base 3.3L) generated enough shift-quality complaints to spawn a 2019 class-action lawsuit. Symptoms: shudder in the 30–45 mph torque-converter lockup zone, harsh 1→2 upshifts, and shift hunting at light throttle. Ford issued multiple calibration reflashes. A truck with the latest Ford tune often improves significantly; an unaddressed 10R80 that still shudders is a negotiation item.
Hold steady throttle at 35–45 mph on flat road and feel for a rhythmic vibration or shudder. Roll on and off throttle lightly at that speed. From a stop, accelerate moderately to 30 mph and feel for a harsh jolt on the 1→2 upshift. Confirm with a Ford dealer whether the latest 10R80 calibration is loaded.
Est. cost: $0 if software reflash resolves / $3,500–$6,000+ if torque converter or valve body replacement needed Source: Ford 10R80 class-action documentation + NHTSA complaint data + Ford Authority coverage

10R80 transmission fluid condition

Monitor
Ford marketed the 10R80 as "fill for life" — that’s a marketing claim, not an engineering specification. Fluid degrades under towing heat. On a truck past 100,000 miles with no fluid service, budget for a drain-and-fill.
Ask whether transmission fluid has been serviced. "Never — Ford says fill for life" on a 100k truck = price in $300–$600 for proper service. Burnt smell or visibly dark fluid = hard signal.
Est. cost: $300–$600 transmission fluid service / $5,000+ rebuild if heat-damaged Source: Ford 10R80 service guidance + industry technician documentation

6-speed automatic on 3.3L Ti-VCT V6 (base trim)

Low Risk
The base 3.3L V6 uses Ford’s conventional six-speed automatic — not the 10R80. If the 10R80’s complaint history concerns you, the base trim with the 6-speed is the lower-risk powertrain combination.
Standard test through all gears: smooth engagement, no hesitation, clean Reverse. Verify ATF service every 60,000 miles.
Est. cost: $150–$250 fluid service / $2,500–$4,000 rebuild if neglected Source: Ford 6R80 transmission documentation

Brake System

Brake master cylinder — NHTSA 25V236000 (open recall check)

Critical — Open Recall
NHTSA recall 25V236000 covers certain 2017–2018 F-150: brake fluid can leak from the front-circuit reservoir into the brake booster, reducing front-brake effectiveness. This is an expansion of prior campaigns 22V150 and 20V332. The dealer remedy is master cylinder replacement at no charge. A 2018 F-150 with this recall still open should not be purchased until the recall work is scheduled.
Enter the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before the test drive and confirm 25V236000 is closed. Signs of active failure: sinking or soft brake pedal, unusually long stopping distances, ABS warning light. If open, require dealer repair before purchase or delivery.
Est. cost: Free dealer repair under recall / Do not drive home with soft pedal Source: NHTSA recall 25V236000 + prior campaigns 22V150 + 20V332

Brake pad and rotor wear

Monitor
Front brake pads on a towing-use F-150 wear faster than on passenger cars. Warped rotors from towing heat show up as pedal pulsation under braking.
Brake firmly from 50 mph — the pedal should be firm and consistent with no vibration. Pulsation or steering shimmer during braking = warped front rotors. Budget brakes as a normal consumable on any truck over 60k.
Est. cost: $400–$800 front pads + rotors Source: F-150 brake wear pattern

Frame, Body & Underbody

Steel frame rust — salt-belt trucks

High Attention
The aluminum body is a genuine rust-resistance feature. The frame is conventional steel and can rust in salt-belt states. Rear sections — behind the rear wheels, spare tire mount, crossmembers, and rear shock mounts — are most vulnerable. Surface flash rust is universal. Scaling, flaking, or through-pitting on structural sections is a safety concern.
On a lift, inspect the rear half of the frame: rear crossmembers, boxed sections behind the rear wheels, spare tire winch mount, and rear shock mounts. Surface rust = cosmetic. Scaled or flaking rust = structural concern. Through-pitting on a crossmember = walk away. Bring a flashlight.
Est. cost: $0 cosmetic surface rust / $1,500–$5,000+ structural frame repair Source: F-150 salt-belt frame inspection pattern

Aluminum body collision repair cost

Monitor
The aluminum body does not rust, but collision repairs cost 2–3× more than equivalent steel repairs. A minor collision that runs $800–$1,200 on a steel truck can cost $2,500–$4,000 on an aluminum-panel F-150. Any accident with structural damage to the bed or cab corners should get a certified aluminum-repair shop estimate.
Walk all four sides in bright sunlight and look for panel color, texture, or sheen mismatches. Check door gap consistency. Inspect the tailgate and bed rail for dents — the highest-cost aluminum panels to replace.
Est. cost: $2,500–$8,000+ aluminum accident repair vs. $800–$2,500 equivalent steel Source: Ford F-150 aluminum body repair cost documentation

Brake lines and fuel lines — underbody (salt-belt)

Monitor
Even on salt-belt trucks where the aluminum body looks pristine, steel brake lines and fuel lines can develop pitting corrosion. Pinhole corrosion in a brake line can cause sudden pressure loss.
On a lift, trace brake lines from the master cylinder to all four corners. Surface rust on steel lines is normal. Pitting or bubbling in the metal wall = replacement required before driving.
Est. cost: $300–$1,500 brake line replacement Source: Salt-belt truck underbody inspection pattern

4x4 system — all modes

Monitor
The electric-shift-on-the-fly 4x4 system uses a vacuum-actuated front axle disconnect and an electronic transfer case. Both need to be exercised on the test drive. A vacuum line failure is a $50 fix; a transfer case actuator is $400–$800.
At low speed in a safe area, cycle through all 4x4 modes: 2WD → 4A → 4H → 4L → 4H → 2WD. Each engagement should be smooth and confirmed by the dash indicator. Any grinding or failure to engage = vacuum line or actuator issue.
Est. cost: $50–$150 vacuum line / $400–$800 transfer case actuator / $1,500+ transfer case Source: F-150 4x4 system common service items

Electrical, Infotainment & Accessories

Door latch sticking or not latching fully

Monitor
Door latch failures are the second-most-common complaint category for the 2018 F-150 on CarComplaints. A door that requires a firm slam, resists opening from inside, or does not click fully shut is a known failure mode. Latches are relatively inexpensive but labor-intensive to replace behind the door panel.
Cycle every door — all four doors plus the tailgate — from inside and outside. Each should open and close with moderate force and latch with a single firm click. A door that needs to be slammed, lifted slightly on closing, or pulled hard to open = latch assembly service needed.
Est. cost: $200–$450 per door latch (parts + labor) Source: NHTSA complaint data + CarComplaints 2018 F-150 door latch category

Sync 3 infotainment — reboot and Bluetooth pairing

Low Risk
Sync 3 on the 2018 F-150 has aged into intermittent reboots and Bluetooth pairing issues. A single reboot on the test drive is not alarming; inability to pair a phone or a frozen screen requiring a full power cycle is a concern.
Pair your phone via Bluetooth before the test drive, confirm audio plays, and verify CarPlay if the trim supports it. Test voice commands. Screen response should be under 1 second per touch. A software update from a Ford dealer resolves most Sync 3 issues at no charge.
Est. cost: $0 software update / $400–$1,200 head unit replacement (last resort) Source: Ford Sync 3 documentation + 2018 F-150 owner reports

Power tailgate — cycle and latch check

Low Risk
Power-up/down tailgates (available on higher trims) can develop motor hesitation, rattles, or latch issues. Cycle the tailgate completely on the test drive.
Cycle the power tailgate from fully closed to fully open and back at least twice. It should travel smoothly and latch with a firm click. Hesitation mid-travel or failure to latch cleanly = motor or latch service.
Est. cost: $200–$600 power tailgate motor or latch service Source: F-150 power tailgate owner reports

Open Recalls

NHTSA 25V236000 — brake master cylinder (covered above)

Critical
Detailed in the Brake System section. Verify this recall is closed before purchase. This is the safety-critical recall on the 2018 F-150.
VIN check at nhtsa.gov/recalls. Any open brake recall = do not finalize purchase until dealer repair is scheduled.
Est. cost: Free dealer repair Source: NHTSA recall 25V236000

Tailgate latch recall — verify VIN

High Attention
Some 2018 F-150 units have had a tailgate latch recall where the gate can open unexpectedly while driving. Not all VINs are affected — confirm against this specific truck’s VIN.
Run the full VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and confirm all open recalls. Any tailgate recall = require dealer completion before delivery. Cycle the tailgate manually: it should resist opening from road vibration when latched.
Est. cost: Free dealer repair Source: NHTSA recall database — F-150 tailgate campaigns

Additional recalls — LED headlamp/DRL, engine heater, tires

Monitor
Additional recall categories affecting some 2018 F-150 production include LED headlamp/DRL software, engine block heater wiring (cold-climate builds), and Continental tire production issues. Lower safety urgency than the brake recall but should be verified as closed.
Print the NHTSA recall report for this VIN before any negotiation. Each open recall is free dealer work the prior owner skipped — and each is a negotiation point.
Est. cost: Free dealer repair on all open recalls Source: NHTSA recall database — 2018 Ford F-150

What the VIN tells you

The 2018 F-150’s 8th VIN digit encodes engine family — five options with meaningfully different failure profiles. Always decode the full VIN at autovetting.com/decode before negotiating. Key split: 3.5L EcoBoost V6 (popular tow rig, most cam phaser complaints) vs. 5.0L Coyote V8 (plastic oil pan leak + spark plug risk) vs. 3.3L Ti-VCT V6 (base, fewest catastrophic failure modes).
