2019 Chevrolet Malibu Pre-Purchase Inspection Checklist: What to Check Before You Buy

By AutoVetting Editorial · Published June 28, 2026

Quick verdict

The 2019 Malibu is one of the cheapest ways into a roomy, comfortable midsize sedan right now — and there's a reason the prices look so good. Chevy discontinued the Malibu, and the used market is flooded with off-lease and ex-rental cars, which pushes asking prices down. The catch is the drivetrain lottery: the volume 1.5-liter turbo trims got a brand-new continuously variable transmission (CVT) for 2019, and that CVT has a well-documented habit of slipping and hesitating. Buy the right trim with clean service history and a clean inspection and the Malibu is a lot of car for the money. Buy the wrong one and you're staring down a five-figure transmission bill. This is a car you do not buy without a pre-purchase inspection.

The 2019 Chevrolet Malibu at a glance

The 2019 model year is the mid-cycle refresh of the ninth-generation Malibu (2016–2025). For used buyers, the single most important thing to understand is that the transmission depends entirely on the trim:

  • L, LS, RS, LT — 1.5-liter turbo four-cylinder (160 hp), paired with a CVT that was new for 2019. This is the high-volume drivetrain and the one with the reputation problem.
  • Premier — 2.0-liter turbo four-cylinder (250 hp), paired with a 9-speed automatic. Quicker, and a conventional geared automatic instead of a CVT.
  • Hybrid — 1.8-liter engine plus a two-motor unit (182 hp combined). 2019 was the final year for the Malibu Hybrid.

All 2019 Malibus are front-wheel drive. The practical takeaway: if you're cross-shopping a base LT against a Premier and can stretch the budget, the Premier's 2.0T/9-speed combination sidesteps the CVT question entirely. And if you find a pre-2019 Malibu (2016–2018) with the 1.5T, note that those used a conventional 6-speed automatic, not the CVT — a meaningfully different reliability story for the same body style.

It's also worth understanding why there are so many 2019 Malibus on the market at attractive prices. The Malibu was a fleet and rental staple in its final years, and with the model now discontinued, those cars are rolling off lease and out of rental fleets in volume. That's good news for your wallet and a reason for extra diligence: a disproportionate share of these were daily-rental cars, driven hard by people who didn't own them, and the cheapest listings are often the ones with the thinnest paper trail. The Malibu Hybrid is the quiet standout of the lineup — 2019 was its last year, it pairs the 1.8-liter engine with a two-motor unit, and it avoids the troublesome 1.5T CVT entirely — but hybrids are rarer on the used lot, so most shoppers will be choosing between 1.5T and 2.0T cars.

Common problems by mileage band

Under 60,000 miles

Most low-mileage 2019 Malibus are ex-fleet or off-lease cars, so the wear is light but the maintenance history is often thin. The thing to watch even on a low-mileage 1.5T-CVT car is transmission behavior at this stage: owners report sluggish, jerky take-off from a stop, a hesitation where power doesn't really arrive until the engine is spinning 2,500–3,000+ rpm, and the occasional shudder. Some of the worst cases have seen a CVT fail well before 60k. On the engine side, the 1.5L turbo can show early signs of carbon buildup and the usual small-turbo cooling-system fussiness, but the gearbox is the headline. Test-drive every 1.5T candidate specifically hunting for slip and hesitation — do not let a salesperson rush the drive.

Beyond the powertrain, low-mileage cars in this band are where you confirm the car wasn't a hard-life rental. Check that all four tires match and are wearing evenly, look for curb rash and panel-gap inconsistencies that hint at quick bodywork, and exercise the infotainment (the Chevrolet MyLink touchscreen can be glitchy), the backup camera, and every power accessory. A car this young should have zero stored fault codes — pull them and treat anything pending as a question to answer, not a detail to ignore.

60,000–100,000 miles

This is the danger zone for the 1.5T CVT. Real-world owner reports include transmissions replaced under warranty and then failing again in the 80k–90k range. The symptom pattern is consistent: delayed engagement, slipping (revs climb but speed doesn't), and in some cases a lost-forward-gear condition with a check-engine or transmission warning light. GM's own service literature traces a common version of this to a clutch regulator valve sticking in the valve body. If a car in this band is out of powertrain warranty and shows any of these symptoms, walk — a CVT replacement is a four-figure-to-five-figure repair. The 2.0T/9-speed Premier is far less drama-prone here; its main needs are routine fluid services and attention to turbo cooling.

Over 100,000 miles

A 100k-plus 1.5T-CVT Malibu is a gamble unless you have proof the transmission was serviced (or replaced) and a clean inspection confirms it shifts properly today. At this mileage you're also into normal high-mile items across all trims: suspension bushings and struts, the 1.5T's PCV/oil-consumption tendencies, water pump and coolant-system components on the turbo engines, and electrical gremlins from the body control module. A high-mile Premier (2.0T/9-speed) or Hybrid with documented maintenance is a far safer six-figure-odometer buy than the 1.5T-CVT volume trims.

Open recalls and TSBs to verify

  • Rear seat belt retractor fasteners — NHTSA campaign 21V649000 (GM number N212333380). Covers 2016–2021 Malibu (and 2019–2021 Cadillac XT4); the rear seat belt retractors may have been improperly secured with loose or missing fasteners, and the fix is an inspect-and-tighten. This is the headline recall touching the 2019 Malibu — confirm by VIN that it was completed.
  • CVT slipping / lost-forward-gear service bulletin. GM service literature describes a clutch regulator valve sticking in the valve body on the CVT, which can cause slipping or a lost-forward-gear condition with a warning light. This is documented as a service bulletin, not a safety recall, so it isn't a free fix — the specific bulletin document number is `[unverified]` here and worth confirming with a dealer or in NHTSA's TSB database for your exact VIN.

Recall campaigns change over time, so always run the specific VIN through NHTSA's recall lookup (or have your inspector do it) rather than trusting a generic year/make/model list.

Red flags to walk away from

  1. Any 1.5T-CVT car that slips, shudders, or hesitates on the test drive — especially out of powertrain warranty. This is the single biggest dealbreaker on this model.
  2. A transmission warning light, or evidence one was recently cleared before you arrived. Ask, and scan for it.
  3. No service records on a high-mileage 1.5T car. Without proof the CVT was maintained or replaced, you're buying blind on the exact component most likely to fail.
  4. An unperformed open recall (e.g., 21V649) the seller "didn't get around to."
  5. Heavy ex-fleet wear masked by a quick detail — mismatched tires, curbed wheels, and interior wear that doesn't match a suspiciously low odometer.

Negotiation leverage

Two facts are on your side as a buyer. First, the Malibu is discontinued, and the market is absorbing a wave of off-lease and ex-rental cars — supply is high, so don't pay top-of-listing money. Second, the CVT reputation is public knowledge, and you can use it. If you're looking at a 1.5T car, price in the real possibility of a future transmission service or replacement and negotiate accordingly; if the inspection turns up any early slip or hesitation, that's hard, documented leverage to drop the price or walk. On a 2.0T Premier, the leverage is softer — but the fleet-glut supply argument still holds. Either way, an inspection that finds even minor issues pays for itself several times over at the negotiating table.
