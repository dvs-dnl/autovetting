2019 Toyota RAV4 Pre-Purchase Inspection Checklist: What to Check Before You Buy

By AutoVetting Editorial · Published July 1, 2026

Quick verdict

The 2019 Toyota RAV4 is one of the safest used compact-SUV bets on the market — but "safe bet" doesn't mean "buy it blind." 2019 was the launch year of the all-new fifth-generation RAV4, and like most first-year redesigns it shipped with a handful of teething problems: a low-speed transmission hesitation that generated hundreds of complaints, a fuel-pump recall that Toyota kept expanding for years, and a maddening fuel-tank quirk. The good news is that almost none of these are engine-killers, and most are fixable with a software update or a recall that should already be done. Land a clean one with the recalls closed out and the RAV4 is exactly the dependable, efficient, resale-proof SUV its reputation promises. Your job before buying is simply to confirm this specific car got the fixes.

The 2019 Toyota RAV4 at a glance

2019 is the first year of the fifth-generation RAV4 (chassis code XA50), built on Toyota's TNGA-K platform. That "all-new" status is the single most important framing for a used buyer: you're getting a genuinely modern RAV4, but also a first-model-year car, so recall and TSB history matters more here than on a mid-cycle model.

There are two very different drivetrains, and knowing which one you're looking at changes the whole inspection:

  • Gas RAV4 — 2.5-liter Dynamic Force four-cylinder (A25A, 203 hp) paired with an 8-speed automatic. This is a conventional geared automatic — not a CVT, despite what a lot of low-quality web pages claim. Trims: LE, XLE, XLE Premium, Adventure, Limited (plus the off-road-leaning Adventure with torque-vectoring AWD).
  • RAV4 Hybrid — 2.5-liter engine plus electric motors (~219 hp combined) driving through an electronically controlled CVT (eCVT) with a dedicated rear electric motor for all-wheel drive (Toyota calls it AWD-i). Trims: LE, XLE, XSE, Limited. The hybrid is quicker to 60 than the gas car and returns roughly 38–40 mpg in real-world driving.

All-wheel drive is available on both, but the systems differ: gas Adventure/Limited models use a mechanical Dynamic Torque Vectoring AWD with a rear driveline disconnect; the hybrid uses the electric rear motor. If you're buying for snow or light trails, confirm which AWD system the car actually has rather than assuming.

The practical takeaway: the gas car's transmission is an 8-speed automatic and the hybrid's is an eCVT, and they have completely different failure profiles. Get that straight before you evaluate any "transmission problem" you read about online.

Common problems by mileage band

Under 60,000 miles

The headline issue in this band is low-speed transmission hesitation on the gas 8-speed cars. Owners filed hundreds of complaints describing a hesitation — then a lurch — when accelerating from a rolling stop or a slow crawl: you press the gas, nothing happens for a beat or two, and then the car jerks forward as the transmission finally grabs a gear. Toyota traced it to shift programming and issued a Technical Service Bulletin (T-SB-0107-19) that reprograms the Engine Control Module; the condition typically shows up at about 6 mph or below, right after the transmission downshifts, under light throttle. This is a software fix, not a mechanical failure — critical distinction. On your test drive, deliberately do several rolling-stop-and-go maneuvers in a parking lot and light-throttle crawls, and feel for the hesitation-then-lurch. If you feel it, it's not a reason to walk — it's a reason to ask whether the ECM reflash was done, and to use it as leverage if it wasn't.

The other big-ticket item at any mileage is the fuel pump recall (20V-012) — see the recalls section — because a failed pump can stall the engine while driving. Many low-mileage cars have already had it done, but you must verify by VIN.

Two nuisance-level issues also cluster early: the fuel tank that won't fill completely (the pump nozzle keeps clicking off at around three-quarters full, forcing you to trickle-fill), and occasional infotainment glitches (Apple CarPlay dropouts, laggy screen). Neither is a safety issue, but the fuel-tank behavior is genuinely annoying and worth confirming on a test fill-up if you can.

60,000–100,000 miles

By this mileage most software and recall fixes should be long done, so the questions shift to maintenance. On the gas 2.5L, this is a direct-injection engine, so ask about intake-side carbon and confirm oil changes were kept on schedule — Toyota's Dynamic Force engines are robust, but sludge and carbon are the enemies of any modern DI four-cylinder that's been neglected. Have the 8-speed automatic's fluid condition checked; Toyota calls it "lifetime" fill, but a fluid check (and a service on a car that tows or lives in heat) is cheap insurance.

On the hybrid, this band is where you listen carefully. The hybrid transaxle and rear motor-generator can develop abnormal noises (a growl or hum) that are worth a trained ear — our inspection protocol flags exactly this on 2019+ RAV4 hybrids. The high-voltage battery itself is generally excellent and carries a long warranty (8 years/100,000 miles federally, longer in some states), but confirm the hybrid system health and that the car hasn't thrown any hybrid-related codes.

Across both, start checking the usual 60k–100k wear items: brakes, tires (all four matching and evenly worn), suspension bushings, and the 12-volt battery, which on hybrids especially can strand you when it dies.

Over 100,000 miles

The RAV4's reputation is built here — a well-maintained 2019 with six-figure miles is usually still a strong car. What you're verifying is that it was actually maintained. Look for a documented service history, evidence the recalls were completed, and no ongoing transmission or hybrid-system complaints. On gas cars, a transmission that still hesitates at low speed at this mileage — with no record of the TSB reflash — is a car whose owner ignored a known fix, which tells you how the rest of the maintenance probably went. On hybrids, get the high-voltage battery state-of-health checked; replacement out of warranty is expensive, and while 2019 batteries are mostly holding up well, "mostly" is not "this one." At this mileage the inspection isn't about catching a design flaw — it's about catching the specific tired example.

Open recalls and TSBs to verify

  • Fuel pump — NHTSA campaign 20V-012 (Toyota code 20TA02). The Denso low-pressure fuel pump inside the tank can fail, and if it does the engine can stall while driving, raising crash risk. It covers certain 2019–2020 RAV4 (among many other Toyota and Lexus models). This one matters because Toyota expanded it repeatedly — the most recent amendment was around January 2025, pulling in additional vehicles built July 2017 through September 2019. The fix is a free fuel-pump-assembly replacement. Because the scope kept growing, do not assume an early "recall complete" note covers the latest amendment — confirm the VIN is clear today.
  • Back-up camera — NHTSA campaign 19V-576 (Toyota code K0N). On certain 2019 RAV4 and RAV4 Hybrid units, a bent pin in the audio display unit can leave the backup camera not activating in reverse, which fails FMVSS 111 "Rear Visibility." Remedy is to inspect and, if needed, replace the audio display unit, free of charge. The recall began September 2019, so most affected cars are fixed — but verify.
  • Low-speed hesitation — TSB T-SB-0107-19. Not a safety recall, so it isn't a guaranteed free repair, but it's the documented fix (an ECM reprogram) for the acceleration hesitation described above. Confirm whether it was performed on a gas 8AT car that hesitates.
  • Fuel tank that won't fill completely. A widely reported 2019–2020 RAV4 condition (the tank stops accepting fuel around three-quarters full). Toyota addressed it through a customer-support/warranty action and, in some cases, a tank replacement. Recall campaigns change over time — as the fuel-pump saga shows — so always run the specific VIN through NHTSA's recall lookup (or have your inspector do it) rather than trusting a generic year/make/model summary.

Red flags to walk away from

  1. An open fuel-pump recall (20V-012) that hasn't been completed, or a "completed" claim that predates the latest amendment. A stalling engine is a safety issue, not a nuisance — insist on VIN confirmation.
  2. A gas car that hesitates badly at low speed with no record of the ECM reflash — fixable, but it signals a neglectful owner and gives you real leverage; if the seller won't budge, walk.
  3. A hybrid with any high-voltage-battery warning, hybrid fault codes, or odd transaxle noise and no health check you can trust.
  4. No maintenance records on a high-mileage car, especially oil-change history on the direct-injection gas engine.
  5. Signs of a hard rental or fleet life hidden under a quick detail — mismatched tires, curb-rashed wheels, and interior wear that doesn't match the odometer.

Negotiation leverage

The RAV4 holds its value stubbornly, so you won't find fire-sale pricing — but you do have specific, documented talking points. If you're looking at a gas car that shows the low-speed hesitation and the seller can't prove the TSB reflash was done, that's a concrete, well-known issue you can name to justify a lower price (and a fix you may have to arrange yourself). If any recall is still open, that's an unperformed safety repair the seller should have handled — price it in or make completion a condition of sale. On hybrids, a battery health check that comes back merely "okay" rather than "excellent" is fair grounds to negotiate given replacement cost. And because so many buyers assume every RAV4 is flawless, simply showing up with an inspection report that lists real findings resets the conversation in your favor.
