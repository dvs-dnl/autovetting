Buying a 2019 Chevrolet Equinox: what to check before you sign

By AutoVetting Editorial · Published June 17, 2026

The 2019 Chevrolet Equinox is one of the most common compact SUVs on the used lot, and that's exactly why it deserves a careful look rather than a casual one. It ranks among the best-selling used vehicles in the country, which means there are thousands of them for sale, at every price and in every condition — well-kept one-owner cars sitting right next to fleet returns and oil-starved bargains. The badge tells you nothing about which one you're standing in front of. The car has to.

This is a genuinely useful, comfortable, easy-to-live-with crossover. It's also a vehicle with a couple of specific, well-documented habits — a turbo engine that some examples burn oil on, a timing chain that can rattle on cold starts, and a small handful of recalls worth confirming by VIN. None of that makes the 2019 Equinox a bad buy. It makes it a car you verify instead of trust.

TL;DR: A 2019 Equinox is a solid used pick if the specific car checks out. The thing to nail down is the 1.5L turbo's oil consumption — bring it home only after you've confirmed the oil level is full and the engine isn't drinking a quart between changes. Confirm the start-stop accumulator recall (NHTSA 20V668) and the rear brake-caliper recall (18V576) show as completed by VIN. If you can find a clean 2.0L turbo LT or Premier with records, that's the connoisseur's pick. Walk away from any example with a milky dipstick, a persistent cold-start rattle, or no maintenance history at this price point.

Quick verdict

Buy it if the oil system checks out and the recalls are closed; it's a comfortable, practical crossover with strong used value. Watch out for the 1.5L turbo's oil-consumption tendency and a possible timing-chain rattle, both of which a 20-minute inspection will surface. The safest configurations are a well-documented 2.0L turbo, or a 1.5L LT whose owner can show you oil-change receipts and a dipstick that reads full.

The 2019 Chevrolet Equinox at a glance

The 2019 model year sits inside the third-generation Equinox (2018–2024), the smaller, lighter redesign Chevy launched for 2018. For used buyers, the most important fork is the engine.

The volume engine is the 1.5L turbocharged four (LYX), rated at 170 hp, paired with a six-speed automatic. It's what you'll find in the L, LS, and most LT examples, and it's the one the oil-consumption conversation is about. The step-up engine is the 2.0L turbocharged four (LTG), around 252 hp, paired with a nine-speed automatic, available on LT and standard on the top Premier trim. The 2.0L is noticeably stronger, tows more, and — relevant here — is generally regarded as the more robust long-haul choice. There was also a 1.6L turbodiesel offered in small numbers; it's a rare bird on the used market and a different maintenance story (DEF system, different service intervals), so most shoppers can set it aside.

Trims run L (fleet-grade base), LS, LT (the sweet spot — available with either engine and the features most buyers want), and Premier (leather, the 2.0L, more driver aids). Front-wheel drive is standard; all-wheel drive is optional across the line and worth having in snow country, though it adds a rear differential and transfer unit to your service checklist.

Common problems by mileage band

The Equinox's issues are mileage-progressive, so where the odometer sits changes what you should be looking for.

Under 60,000 miles

At lower mileage the headline item is oil consumption on the 1.5L turbo. Some engines use noticeably more oil than you'd expect — owners report needing to top up between changes. It isn't universal, but it's common enough that it should be the first thing you check on any 1.5L car: pull the dipstick on a cold engine and confirm it reads full, and ask the seller how often they add oil. An engine that's already low at 40,000–50,000 miles is telling you something.

Also in this band: the occasional PCV / crankcase-pressure issue, which shows up as oil weeping near the dipstick tube or the top of the engine. It's often a faulty PCV valve creating excess crankcase pressure rather than a catastrophic failure, but it's related to the oil-consumption picture and worth flagging. A scattering of check-engine lights tied to sensors also appears in this range — always scan the car for stored and pending codes before you buy, even if no light is currently on.

60,000–100,000 miles

This is where the timing chain earns attention. A rattle on cold start-up — a brief metallic clatter in the first second or two before oil pressure builds — can indicate chain stretch. GM has issued a service bulletin related to this symptom [unverified TSB ID — confirm with a dealer by VIN], and addressing it isn't cheap, so a persistent cold-start rattle is real negotiating leverage or a reason to keep shopping. The honest test is a true cold start: get to the car before it's been warmed up, or ask the seller not to start it before you arrive.

Oil consumption that began earlier tends to be more pronounced by now, so the dipstick check matters even more. Start paying closer attention to transmission behavior too — both the six-speed (1.5L) and nine-speed (2.0L) should shift cleanly without harshness, flaring, or hunting. Any shudder or delayed engagement is worth a transmission-fluid service check and a closer look. Check service records for fluid changes around this interval.

Over 100,000 miles

Past 100,000 miles the 2019 Equinox can be a perfectly good buy — the third generation earns above-average owner reliability marks — but only with a maintenance trail behind it. At this mileage you're verifying that the earlier issues were managed rather than ignored: an engine that's been topped up and serviced is fine; one that's been run low on oil for years is a future repair bill. Look closely for oil leaks (valve cover, oil cooler, the dipstick-tube area), suspension wear (clunks over bumps, worn struts and bushings), and the usual high-mileage consumables — brakes, tires, battery. On AWD cars, confirm the rear differential and transfer-unit fluids were actually serviced; that interval gets skipped constantly.

Open recalls and TSBs to verify

Always confirm recall status by VIN at the seller, a dealer, or NHTSA — recalls are remedied per-vehicle, and a model-year recall list only tells you what might apply. For the 2019 Equinox, the ones to check:

  • NHTSA 20V668 (GM recall N202313440) — start-stop accumulator. The start-stop accumulator endcap may have missing bolts, which can cause a transmission fluid leak and, in the worst case, a loss of propulsion (and a fire risk if fluid reaches an ignition source). It covers 2018–2020 Equinox along with many GM siblings; the remedy began in December 2020. This is the most consequential one to confirm closed.
  • NHTSA 18V576 — rear brake calipers. Rear caliper pistons with insufficient coating can form gas pockets and reduce rear braking performance. It covers 2018–2019 Equinox and a long list of other GM models; the fix is a brake-system bleed.
  • NHTSA 19V184 — rear-right head restraint. Insufficient welds on the rear-right seat head-restraint bracket can reduce protection in a crash.
  • Fuel-tank seam leak. Some sources list a recall for an improperly sealed fuel-tank seam that could allow a leak near the fuel inlet and raise fire risk. We could not confirm the campaign number this pass [unverified] — treat it as one more reason to run the VIN and let the recall lookup tell you what genuinely applies.

On the bulletin side, the cold-start timing-chain rattle is associated with a GM service bulletin; have a dealer confirm whether one applies to the specific VIN, since the exact bulletin ID should be verified rather than taken from a buyer's guide.

Red flags to walk away from

A few findings are worth more than a price negotiation — they're reasons to keep looking:

  • A milky or "latte"-colored dipstick or coolant, or a low oil level the seller can't explain. On the 1.5L especially, an engine that's been run chronically low is a gamble.
  • A persistent cold-start rattle that doesn't clear in a second or two — possible timing-chain stretch, and an expensive one.
  • No maintenance history at all on a high-mileage 1.5L car. This is the one engine where the paper trail is the product.
  • Transmission shudder, flaring, or harsh/delayed shifts on the test drive, particularly paired with the accumulator recall showing not completed.
  • A check-engine light the seller "just needs to reset" — scan it yourself and find out what's actually stored before you believe any explanation.

Negotiation leverage

The known issues are also your best bargaining chips, because they're documented and specific. If the oil-consumption check shows any tendency to drink oil, that's a real, ongoing cost you can put on the table. A cold-start rattle points at a potentially pricey timing-chain job — concrete leverage, or a reason to walk. Any open recall (especially 20V668) is a free repair the seller hasn't bothered to schedule, which tells you something about how the car's been cared for and gives you room to ask for a better price or for the work to be done before sale. And on a 100k-plus AWD example, missing rear-differential and transfer-case service is deferred maintenance you'll inherit — price it in. The cleanest leverage of all is a written inspection report listing exactly what the car needs; it's hard to argue with a measured brake-pad thickness or a logged oil level.
